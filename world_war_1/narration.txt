Sarajevo. The twenty-eighth of June, 1914.
It is a bright Sunday morning. Crowds line the streets. Flags hang from the windows.
An open-topped car rolls slowly along the river. Inside sits Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, and his wife, Sophie.
Hidden in the crowd is a group of young assassins. They carry pistols, bombs, and small capsules of poison.
One of them throws a bomb. It bounces off the back of the car and explodes under the vehicle behind.
The Archduke survives. The plan has failed.
One of the assassins, a nineteen-year-old student named Gavrilo Princip, walks away disappointed. He stops near a small café.
And then, by pure chance, the Archduke's car takes a wrong turn.
The driver stops. He tries to reverse. The engine stalls.
Right in front of Gavrilo Princip.
Princip steps forward, raises his pistol, and fires two shots.
Within minutes, the Archduke and his wife are dead.
Two bullets. Fired by a teenager. On a street corner where a car happened to stop.
Those two bullets would start a war that killed more than fifteen million people, destroyed four empires, and changed the world forever.
This is the story of the First World War.

To understand how two bullets could set the world on fire, we have to look at Europe in 1914.
On the surface, it was a golden age. Railways crossed every country. Cities glowed with electric light. Trade had never been greater.
But underneath, Europe was a powder keg.
The great powers were rivals. Britain had the largest navy and the largest empire on Earth. Germany, a young and ambitious nation, wanted its own "place in the sun." France had never forgotten losing the provinces of Alsace and Lorraine to Germany in 1871. Russia saw itself as the protector of the Slavic peoples of the Balkans. And Austria-Hungary, an old and fragile empire, feared that rising nationalism would tear it apart.
For years, they had been building armies, building battleships, and building alliances.
On one side stood the Triple Entente: France, Russia and Britain.
On the other stood the Central Powers: Germany and Austria-Hungary.
These alliances were meant to keep the peace. If anyone attacked, they would face a wall of enemies.
Instead, they turned a local quarrel into a chain reaction.

Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia for the assassination. The killers had been armed by a secret Serbian nationalist group called the Black Hand.
Germany promised Austria its full support. Historians call it the "blank cheque."
On the twenty-third of July, Austria sent Serbia an ultimatum so harsh it was almost designed to be refused.
Serbia accepted almost everything. Almost.
It wasn't enough.
On the twenty-eighth of July, 1914, exactly one month after the assassination, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.
Then the dominoes began to fall.
Russia began to mobilise its huge army to protect Serbia.
Germany saw Russia's mobilisation as a threat, and declared war on Russia on the first of August.
Two days later, Germany declared war on France, Russia's ally.
But Germany had a problem. It now faced enemies on two sides.
Years earlier, its generals had prepared a solution: the Schlieffen Plan.
The idea was simple and ruthless. Knock France out quickly, in about six weeks, by swinging a massive army through neutral Belgium. Then turn east and deal with slow, giant Russia.
On the fourth of August, German troops crossed into Belgium.
Britain had promised to protect Belgian neutrality in a treaty signed decades before. The German chancellor was astonished that Britain would go to war over what he called "a scrap of paper."
But that same day, Britain declared war on Germany.
In just one week, a murder in Sarajevo had dragged all the great powers of Europe into war.

And here is the strangest part.
Across Europe, crowds cheered.
In Berlin, in Paris, in London, young men rushed to sign up. They sang in the streets. They threw flowers at marching soldiers.
Most people believed the war would be short. Glorious. An adventure.
"Home before the leaves fall," the Kaiser told his troops.
"Over by Christmas," people said in Britain.
They could not have been more wrong.

At first, the Schlieffen Plan seemed to work.
German armies smashed through Belgium. The Belgian fortresses were pounded by enormous siege guns. Refugees fled down every road.
The small British army fought bravely at Mons, but was forced to retreat. The French threw themselves at the Germans in bright blue coats and red trousers, and were cut down in tens of thousands.
By early September, the Germans were less than fifty kilometres from Paris.
The French government fled the capital.
Then, at the River Marne, the French and British turned and fought.
The French were so desperate for men that they commandeered Paris taxis to rush soldiers to the front. Hundreds of cabs carried thousands of troops through the night, meters still running.
The Germans were exhausted, far from their supplies, and their armies had drifted apart, leaving a gap between them.
The Allies struck into that gap.
The Battle of the Marne stopped the German advance and pushed them back.
The Schlieffen Plan had failed. There would be no quick victory.

What happened next would define the entire war.
Both sides tried to outflank each other, racing north towards the sea. Neither could get around the other.
So they did the only thing they could. They dug.
By the end of 1914, a continuous line of trenches stretched for more than seven hundred kilometres, from the coast of Belgium all the way to the border of Switzerland.
The Western Front was born.
And for almost four years, it would barely move.

Life in the trenches was a nightmare.
Soldiers lived in narrow ditches, often knee-deep in freezing water. Rats as big as cats fed on scraps, and on the dead. Lice crawled through every uniform.
Many men developed "trench foot," their feet rotting inside wet boots.
The smell of mud, smoke and death was everywhere.
Between the two lines of trenches lay a strip of ground called No Man's Land. Shell holes. Barbed wire. Shattered trees. Bodies that could not be recovered.
To cross it meant facing machine guns that could fire hundreds of bullets a minute.
The generals had been trained for a war of cavalry charges and bold manoeuvres. But this was a new kind of war. A war of industry. A war of machines.
And the machines were winning.

Yet even in this hell, there were moments of humanity.
On Christmas Eve, 1914, along parts of the front, British soldiers heard something unexpected coming from the German trenches.
Singing. "Stille Nacht." Silent Night.
Small Christmas trees with candles appeared along the German parapets.
Slowly, carefully, men climbed out of their trenches. Enemies met in No Man's Land. They shook hands. They swapped cigarettes, chocolate and buttons. In some places, they even played football.
For a few hours, the war simply stopped.
It became known as the Christmas Truce.
The generals were furious. It would never be allowed to happen on that scale again.
And the next day, the killing resumed.

Meanwhile, in the east, the war looked very different.
Russia attacked much faster than Germany expected, invading East Prussia in August 1914.
But at the Battle of Tannenberg, two German commanders, Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff, surrounded a Russian army and destroyed it. Around ninety thousand Russians were taken prisoner. The Russian commander, unable to face the defeat, took his own life.
The Eastern Front was vast and open. Armies advanced and retreated hundreds of kilometres. But the losses were just as horrifying.
And the war kept spreading.
The Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers. Japan joined the Allies. Fighting broke out in Africa, in the Middle East, on the oceans.
This was no longer a European war.
It was a world war.

And soldiers came from every corner of the globe.
Britain's empire sent men from Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Caribbean and Africa.
But the largest volunteer army of all came from India.
More than one million Indian soldiers served overseas during the war.
In the autumn of 1914, Indian troops arrived in France just as the British army was running out of men. They were rushed straight into the freezing trenches around Ypres, in thin summer uniforms, thousands of kilometres from home.
They fought in France and Belgium, in Egypt and Palestine, in East Africa, at Gallipoli, and in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq.
Indian soldiers won the Victoria Cross, Britain's highest award for bravery. The first went to Khudadad Khan, a machine gunner who kept firing alone after the rest of his team had been killed.
More than seventy thousand Indian soldiers never came home.
Today many of their names are carved on the India Gate in New Delhi, a monument built to remember them.

By 1915, both sides were desperate to break the deadlock.
On the twenty-second of April, near the Belgian town of Ypres, French and Algerian soldiers saw a strange greenish-yellow cloud drifting towards them across No Man's Land.
It was chlorine gas.
Men choked. Their lungs burned. Those who could, ran. A huge gap opened in the Allied line.
Poison gas had arrived on the battlefield.
Soon both sides were using it, chlorine, then phosgene, then mustard gas, which blistered the skin and blinded its victims.
Gas masks became part of every soldier's equipment. The war had found a new way to terrify.

Britain, too, looked for a way around the stalemate.
A young politician named Winston Churchill, in charge of the Royal Navy, had a bold idea.
Instead of attacking Germany head-on, attack its weakest ally, the Ottoman Empire. Force a way through the narrow straits of the Dardanelles, capture Constantinople, and open a supply route to Russia.
On the twenty-fifth of April, 1915, British, French, Australian and New Zealand troops landed on the Gallipoli peninsula.
It was a disaster.
The Ottoman defenders, led by a determined officer named Mustafa Kemal, held the high ground. The Allied troops were pinned to narrow beaches and steep cliffs.
For eight months they clung on, in scorching heat, then freezing cold, plagued by flies and disease.
In January 1916, the Allies finally withdrew.
Hundreds of thousands of men on both sides had been killed or wounded.
For Australia and New Zealand, Gallipoli became a defining moment in their national story. The twenty-fifth of April is still remembered there as Anzac Day.
And Mustafa Kemal would later become Atatürk, the founder of modern Turkey.

At sea, Britain's navy blockaded Germany, slowly starving it of food and supplies.
Germany struck back with a new weapon: the submarine. The U-boat.
On the seventh of May, 1915, a German U-boat torpedoed the British ocean liner Lusitania off the coast of Ireland.
She sank in just eighteen minutes.
Nearly twelve hundred people died, including more than a hundred Americans.
The United States was outraged. But for now, it stayed out of the war.

Then came 1916. The year of the great slaughters.
In February, Germany attacked the French fortress city of Verdun.
The German commander, Erich von Falkenhayn, had a chilling plan. He did not just want to capture Verdun. He wanted to force France to defend it at any cost, and to "bleed France white."
The battle lasted almost the whole year.
Millions of shells turned forests and villages into a lunar landscape. Whole villages vanished from the map.
The French vowed: "They shall not pass."
And they did not. Verdun held.
But the cost was staggering. Around seven hundred thousand men were killed or wounded, French and German, for a few kilometres of ground.

To take pressure off Verdun, the British launched their own great offensive, along the River Somme.
For a week, British guns fired more than a million and a half shells at the German lines. Surely, the generals thought, nothing could survive that.
On the morning of the first of July, 1916, whistles blew along the British trenches. Men climbed out and began to walk across No Man's Land.
But the Germans had survived, deep in their underground bunkers. And as the shelling stopped, they ran up their stairs and set up their machine guns.
On that single day, the British Army suffered around fifty-seven thousand casualties. Nearly twenty thousand men were killed.
It remains the bloodiest day in British military history.
The Battle of the Somme dragged on until November. In September, a strange new machine appeared on the battlefield for the first time. A metal box on caterpillar tracks, armed with guns, crawling over the trenches.
To keep it secret, the British had called it a "water tank."
The name stuck. The tank had been born.
By the end of the Somme, more than one million men had been killed or wounded. The Allies had advanced about ten kilometres.

In the east, that same summer, Russia struck one more great blow.
General Aleksei Brusilov attacked along a huge front instead of at a single point, with short, sudden bombardments and fast-moving assault troops.
The Austro-Hungarian army was caught by surprise and crumbled. Hundreds of thousands of men surrendered.
It was one of the most successful Allied attacks of the entire war. Austria-Hungary never fully recovered.
But Russia's own losses were enormous. And at home, Russians were losing faith in the war, and in their Tsar.

The war was also fought in the skies.
At first, aircraft were used only to scout enemy positions. Pilots waved at each other. Then they started throwing bricks. Then firing pistols. Then mounting machine guns.
The age of the fighter pilot had begun.
Aces became celebrities. The most famous of all was a German pilot, Manfred von Richthofen, who painted his plane bright red.
They called him the Red Baron.
He shot down eighty enemy aircraft before he himself was killed in April 1918.
But most pilots did not survive long. In some periods, a new pilot's life expectancy at the front could be measured in weeks.

And at sea, in May 1916, the two great battle fleets finally met, at the Battle of Jutland in the North Sea.
It was the largest naval battle of the war. Britain lost more ships and more men. But the German fleet returned to port, and almost never challenged the Royal Navy again.
The blockade continued. And in Germany, people began to go hungry.

Because this was a total war, it was not only fought by soldiers.
Entire nations were turned into war machines.
Factories that once made cars, toys and sewing machines now made shells, rifles and uniforms.
With millions of men at the front, women took their places. They worked in munitions factories, drove buses and ambulances, ran farms and offices. In Britain, women filling shells with explosives were called "munitionettes." The chemicals turned their skin yellow, and people nicknamed them "canaries."
Governments used propaganda on a scale the world had never seen. Posters were everywhere. In Britain, the stern face of Lord Kitchener stared out, pointing his finger: "Your country needs you."
Food was rationed. Newspapers were censored. Letters from the front were checked, and anything too honest was blacked out.
In Germany, the British blockade was so effective that the winter of 1916 became known as the "turnip winter." Potatoes had run out, and families survived on turnips that were usually fed to animals.
At home and at the front, the whole of society was now at war.

By 1917, Europe was exhausted.
Soldiers were tired. Civilians were tired. Food was running out. Strikes and protests spread.
In the French army, after yet another failed offensive, whole units refused to attack. The mutinies were quietly put down, and the army was nursed back to health.
But in Russia, the exhaustion turned into revolution.
The Russian army had lost millions of men. At home, there was hunger, chaos and fury.
In March 1917, protests in Petrograd turned into a revolution. Tsar Nicholas the Second was forced to abdicate. Three hundred years of Romanov rule came to an end.
Germany saw an opportunity.
It secretly helped a Russian revolutionary living in exile in Switzerland to return home, travelling across Germany in a sealed train.
His name was Vladimir Lenin.
In November 1917, Lenin and his Bolsheviks seized power. They promised the Russian people "peace, land and bread."
Soon after, Russia made peace with Germany, giving up huge territories in the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk.
The Eastern Front was closed.
Now Germany could move hundreds of thousands of soldiers to the west.

On other fronts, the war was just as brutal.
Italy had joined the Allies in 1915, hoping to win land from Austria-Hungary. Its soldiers fought in the high mountains of the Alps, where men froze to death, and avalanches killed thousands.
In October 1917, at a place called Caporetto, German and Austrian troops broke through the Italian lines. The Italian army collapsed and retreated more than a hundred kilometres. Hundreds of thousands were captured.
But Italy did not surrender. It held a new line along the River Piave, and fought on.
In the deserts of the Middle East, the war took a very different shape.
The Arabs rose up against Ottoman rule. Fighting alongside them was a young British officer named T. E. Lawrence.
Wearing Arab robes and riding camels, Lawrence and the Arab forces blew up railway lines and attacked Ottoman outposts. In 1917, they captured the port of Aqaba after a daring march across the desert.
He became a legend: Lawrence of Arabia.
Meanwhile, British and Indian troops pushed north from Egypt and captured Jerusalem in December 1917.

But in 1917, Germany also made a fatal mistake.
To starve Britain into surrender, it announced unrestricted submarine warfare. Any ship near Britain, even from neutral countries, could be sunk without warning.
Then the British intercepted and decoded a secret German message, the Zimmermann Telegram.
In it, Germany offered Mexico an alliance. If America entered the war, Mexico should attack the United States, and Germany would help it win back Texas, New Mexico and Arizona.
When the telegram was published, Americans were furious.
On the sixth of April, 1917, the United States declared war on Germany.
America's army was still small. It would take months to train and ship its soldiers across the Atlantic.
But America had something no other country had left: almost unlimited men, money and factories.
The clock was now ticking for Germany.

Meanwhile, on the Western Front, the slaughter went on.
In the second half of 1917, the British attacked near Ypres, at a place whose name became a symbol of the war's misery: Passchendaele.
It rained and rained. The shelling had destroyed the drainage of the low Flemish fields. The battlefield turned into an ocean of mud.
Men and horses slipped off wooden duckboards and drowned in shell holes full of liquid mud.
After more than three months, the village of Passchendaele was finally captured. Its ruins were barely visible.
Hundreds of thousands had fallen.

By the spring of 1918, Germany knew it had one last chance.
With Russia out of the war, it could finally outnumber the Allies in the west. But only until the Americans arrived in large numbers.
Ludendorff decided to gamble everything on one massive attack.
On the twenty-first of March, 1918, the German Spring Offensive began.
After a short but devastating bombardment, specially trained "stormtroopers" slipped through the Allied lines in small groups, bypassing strong points and striking deep.
For the first time in years, the front moved. The Germans advanced dozens of kilometres. They shelled Paris with a giant long-range gun from more than a hundred kilometres away.
The Allies were close to breaking.
But the German army had lost its best soldiers in the attacks. Its men were exhausted and hungry. When they captured Allied supply depots, some stopped to eat and drink, amazed by how much food the enemy still had.
And the Americans were now arriving. Ten thousand of them every single day.
In July, at the Second Battle of the Marne, the German advance was stopped.
The gamble had failed.

On the eighth of August, 1918, the Allies struck back near Amiens, with hundreds of tanks, aircraft, and infantry working together.
The German lines collapsed. Thousands of German soldiers surrendered.
Ludendorff called it "the black day of the German army."
It was the beginning of the Hundred Days Offensive. Day after day, the Allies pushed forward.

Animals, too, played their part in this war.
Millions of horses, mules and donkeys hauled guns and supplies through the mud. Dogs carried messages and searched for the wounded. Cats and terriers hunted rats in the trenches.
And pigeons carried messages when telephone lines were cut.
In October 1918, an American battalion was trapped behind German lines, and was being shelled by its own artillery by mistake. Their last hope was a pigeon named Cher Ami.
She was shot through the chest, lost an eye and most of one leg. But she still flew twenty-five miles in about twenty-five minutes, and delivered the message.
The shelling stopped. Almost two hundred men were saved.

Germany's allies began to fall. Bulgaria surrendered. Then the Ottoman Empire. Then Austria-Hungary began to fall apart.
In Germany itself, sailors mutinied. Workers went on strike. Revolution spread through the streets.
On the ninth of November, Kaiser Wilhelm the Second abdicated and fled into exile in the Netherlands.
Germany was now a republic. And it asked for peace.

In the early hours of the eleventh of November, 1918, in a railway carriage parked in a forest near Compiègne, in France, German officials signed the armistice.
The fighting would stop at eleven o'clock that morning.
The eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month.
But even on that last morning, men kept fighting and dying.
At one minute before eleven, an American soldier named Henry Gunther charged at a German machine gun position. The Germans, knowing the war was almost over, tried to wave him away.
He kept running. They fired.
Henry Gunther is believed to be the last soldier killed in the First World War.
One minute later, the guns fell silent.
Along the front, soldiers on both sides climbed out of their trenches. Some cheered. Some cried. Many simply sat in silence, unable to believe it was really over.
In London, Paris and New York, huge crowds celebrated in the streets.
After four years, three months and two weeks, the Great War was over.

But the world that emerged was not the world that had gone to war.
Around ten million soldiers had been killed. Millions of civilians had died too. Over twenty million people had been wounded. An entire generation of young men had been scarred, many of them for life.
Soldiers came home with missing limbs, with faces destroyed by shrapnel, and with a condition doctors called "shell shock," what we would now understand as severe trauma.
And as the war ended, another killer swept across the world. The Spanish flu pandemic of 1918 killed tens of millions more, spreading through crowded army camps and troopships to every continent.

Four great empires had collapsed.
The German Empire. The Austro-Hungarian Empire. The Russian Empire. The Ottoman Empire.
From their ruins, new countries appeared on the map. Poland. Czechoslovakia. Yugoslavia. Austria and Hungary as separate states. In the Middle East, Britain and France drew new borders, many of which still shape conflicts today.
In 1919, the victorious Allies met at the Palace of Versailles, near Paris, to decide Germany's fate.
The Treaty of Versailles was signed on the twenty-eighth of June, 1919. Exactly five years to the day after the shots in Sarajevo.
Germany lost territory, its colonies, most of its army and navy. It was forced to accept blame for the war and to pay enormous reparations.
Many Germans felt humiliated and betrayed.
Among the bitter veterans was a former corporal who had been wounded and gassed on the Western Front.
His name was Adolf Hitler.
Barely twenty years later, the world would be at war again.

People at the time called it "the war to end all wars."
It wasn't.
But the First World War changed almost everything.
It brought tanks, aircraft, submarines and chemical weapons onto the battlefield. It pulled women into factories and offices in huge numbers, and in many countries helped them win the right to vote. It shattered old empires and old certainties, and gave birth to the modern world.
Today, on the eleventh of November, people in many countries still fall silent at eleven o'clock.
In Britain and the Commonwealth, they wear red poppies, the flowers that bloomed among the graves on the shattered fields of Flanders.
A Canadian doctor, John McCrae, wrote about them in 1915, after burying a friend:
"In Flanders fields the poppies blow, between the crosses, row on row."
It all began with two bullets, on a sunny Sunday morning in Sarajevo.
And it ended with millions of crosses, row on row, across the fields of Europe.
This was the First World War.
