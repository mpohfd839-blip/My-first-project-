Belgium. The eighteenth of June, 1815.
It rained all night. The fields are mud. The soldiers are soaked, hungry, and exhausted.
On one side of the valley stands an army of British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops, under the Duke of Wellington.
On the other side stands the man who has terrified Europe for almost twenty years.
Napoleon Bonaparte.
He has crowned himself Emperor. He has redrawn the map of a continent. He has been defeated, exiled, and has come back.
And today, near a small village called Waterloo, everything he built will be decided in a single afternoon.
But to understand how Europe arrived at this muddy field, we have to go back. Back to a revolution, a small island, and a young officer nobody had heard of.
This is the story of the Napoleonic Wars.

In 1789, France explodes.
The French Revolution topples the old order. The king loses his power, and in 1793, he loses his head.
The monarchies of Europe watch in horror. If kings can fall in Paris, they can fall anywhere.
So Austria, Prussia, Britain, Spain and others go to war against revolutionary France.
France is surrounded. Its old aristocratic officers have fled or been executed. The army is chaos.
And chaos is where opportunity lives.
Napoleon Bonaparte was born in 1769 on Corsica, an island France had taken over only a year earlier. He spoke French with an accent. He was short of money, short of connections, and long on ambition.
In 1793, at the port of Toulon, the British had seized the harbour. A twenty-four-year-old artillery captain studied the map, picked the right hill, and placed his cannons exactly where they would hurt most.
The British fleet was forced out. The young captain was promoted to general.
Two years later, in 1795, a royalist mob marched on the government in Paris. Napoleon met them with cannon fire, what he called a "whiff of grapeshot." The revolt was crushed.
Now the government owed him.
In 1796, they gave him command of a ragged, unpaid army on the Italian border. His soldiers had no boots. Some had no muskets.
He told them: "I will lead you into the most fertile plains in the world."
And he did.
In one campaign, he beat the Austrians again and again, at Lodi, at Arcole, at Rivoli. He moved faster than his enemies thought possible. He struck before they could combine.
By 1797, Austria asked for peace. Napoleon was twenty-seven years old, and he was the most famous man in France.

Britain remained at war with France. Britain was protected by the sea, and the sea belonged to the Royal Navy.
So Napoleon proposed something bold. Strike at Britain's wealth. Strike at the road to India.
In 1798, he sailed for Egypt with an army, and with scientists, artists and scholars.
On land, he won. At the Battle of the Pyramids, his squares of infantry shattered the Mamluk cavalry.
But at sea, disaster.
At Aboukir Bay, the British admiral Horatio Nelson found the French fleet at anchor and destroyed it. The Battle of the Nile left Napoleon's army trapped in Egypt.
His scholars, though, made history of their own. One of their finds was a black stone carved with three scripts. The Rosetta Stone would one day unlock the secrets of Egyptian hieroglyphs.
In 1799, Napoleon heard that France was in trouble. Its government was weak, divided and unpopular.
So he slipped away from Egypt, left his army behind, and sailed for home.
Within weeks, in November 1799, he took part in a coup. The government fell. A new one rose, led by three consuls.
Napoleon was First Consul.
In practice, he was the ruler of France.

Austria was back in the war. So Napoleon did something Hannibal had done: he crossed the Alps.
In June 1800, at Marengo in northern Italy, he nearly lost. By afternoon his army was retreating.
Then fresh troops arrived, a counterattack hit the Austrians, and defeat became victory.
Peace followed with Austria in 1801. Then, in 1802, even Britain signed a peace, the Treaty of Amiens.
For a brief moment, Europe was quiet.
Napoleon used that moment to rebuild France.
He reformed taxes. He founded a national bank. He made peace with the Catholic Church. He created schools for future officers and officials.
And in 1804 came the work he was proudest of: the Civil Code. The Napoleonic Code.
One set of laws for the whole country. Equality before the law for men. Protection of property. Careers open to talent, not to birth.
Parts of it still shape legal systems around the world today.
But the peace did not last. By 1803, Britain and France were at war again.
And on the second of December, 1804, in the cathedral of Notre-Dame, Napoleon did something no one expected.
The Pope had come to crown him. Instead, Napoleon took the crown and placed it on his own head.
The Revolution had executed a king. Now it had an Emperor.

Napoleon gathered a huge army on the Channel coast, near Boulogne. Its target was England.
But to cross the Channel, he needed control of the sea, even for just a few days.
He never got it.
On the twenty-first of October, 1805, off Cape Trafalgar in Spain, Nelson's fleet met the combined fleets of France and Spain.
Nelson sent a signal to his ships: "England expects that every man will do his duty."
The British broke the enemy line. By evening, the French and Spanish fleet was shattered.
Nelson himself was shot and killed during the battle. But Britain would rule the seas for the rest of the war.
Napoleon could not reach Britain.
So he turned east.
Austria and Russia had joined Britain in a new coalition. Napoleon marched his army, now called the Grande Armée, from the coast to the Danube at astonishing speed.
At Ulm, in October 1805, he surrounded an entire Austrian army. It surrendered almost without a fight.
Then came his masterpiece.
On the second of December, 1805, exactly one year after his coronation, near the village of Austerlitz, Napoleon faced the combined armies of Austria and Russia, with both of their emperors present.
He deliberately made his right flank look weak. The allies took the bait and attacked it.
As they did, they emptied the high ground in the centre, the Pratzen Heights.
Out of the morning fog, the sun broke through, and French troops stormed up the hill and split the allied army in two.
It became known as the Battle of the Three Emperors. And it was a total French victory.
Austria made peace. The thousand-year-old Holy Roman Empire was dissolved soon after.
Napoleon was at the height of his genius.

In 1806, Prussia, proud of the army of Frederick the Great, declared war.
The war lasted only weeks.
On a single day, the fourteenth of October, 1806, the French won twin battles at Jena and Auerstedt. The Prussian army collapsed. Napoleon rode into Berlin.
From Berlin, he launched a new kind of war, an economic one.
If he could not beat Britain at sea, he would strangle its trade. Through the Continental System, he ordered all of Europe to stop buying British goods.
Then Russia. In the frozen snow of Eylau, in February 1807, the two armies fought a brutal, bloody draw.
But in June, at Friedland, Napoleon crushed the Russians.
Tsar Alexander the First agreed to talk.
At Tilsit, in July 1807, the two emperors met on a raft in the middle of the Niemen River. They divided Europe between them.
Prussia lost huge territories. Napoleon's brothers became kings. Joseph in Naples. Louis in Holland. Jérôme in Westphalia.
From Spain to Poland, from the North Sea to Italy, Europe now bent to one man.
It seemed nothing could stop him.
But the seeds of his downfall had already been planted.

Portugal refused to obey the Continental System. So in 1807, French troops marched through Spain to take it.
Then Napoleon went further. He pushed aside the Spanish royal family and made his brother Joseph King of Spain.
The Spanish people rose up.
On the second of May, 1808, crowds in Madrid fought French soldiers in the streets. The French answered with mass executions. The painter Francisco Goya would later turn that horror into some of the most powerful images in art.
The revolt spread across the country. Small bands of fighters ambushed convoys, killed messengers and vanished into the hills.
They called it "guerrilla," the little war. That is where the word comes from.
Britain sent an army to Portugal under a general named Arthur Wellesley, the future Duke of Wellington.
For six years, Spain became a trap that swallowed hundreds of thousands of French soldiers. Napoleon later called it his "Spanish ulcer."
Meanwhile, in 1809, Austria tried again.
At Aspern-Essling, near Vienna, Napoleon suffered his first major defeat in over a decade.
But six weeks later, at Wagram, he won a huge and costly victory. Austria made peace once more.
To secure his dynasty, Napoleon divorced his beloved Joséphine, who had given him no children. In 1810 he married Marie-Louise, the daughter of the Austrian Emperor.
In 1811, she gave birth to a son. Napoleon named him King of Rome.
The empire now had an heir.
The future looked secure.
It wasn't.

Tsar Alexander was tired of the Continental System. It was hurting Russia's trade. He began to deal with Britain again.
Napoleon decided to teach him a lesson.
In June 1812, he gathered the largest army Europe had ever seen. Somewhere around half a million men, French, German, Polish, Italian and more, crossed the Niemen River into Russia.
Napoleon expected a quick battle and a quick peace.
The Russians refused to give him one.
They retreated, and retreated, and retreated. They burned crops and villages behind them. The French marched deeper into an empty land, through heat, dust and disease.
Men died of exhaustion, hunger and typhus before they ever saw a battle.
Finally, on the seventh of September, at Borodino, the Russians stood and fought.
It was one of the bloodiest single days in history. Tens of thousands of men fell on both sides.
The Russians withdrew. Napoleon claimed victory. A week later, he entered Moscow.
He found it almost empty.
Then Moscow began to burn. Fires spread through the wooden city, and most of it was destroyed.
Napoleon waited for the Tsar to ask for peace.
The message never came.
In October, with winter approaching, he ordered the retreat.
What followed is one of the great disasters of military history.
Snow. Freezing cold. No food. Cossacks attacking stragglers. Horses dying, guns abandoned, men freezing where they slept.
At the Berezina River in November, French engineers stood in icy water to build bridges so the army could escape. Many of them died doing it.
Of the vast army that had marched into Russia, only a small fraction ever came back.
Napoleon rushed back to Paris ahead of the survivors.
The myth of the unbeatable Emperor was broken.

Now everyone turned against him.
Prussia, Russia, Austria, Sweden and Britain formed a new coalition.
Napoleon raised a new army, mostly young conscripts, and still won battles, at Lützen and at Bautzen in 1813.
But in October 1813, near Leipzig, he was surrounded.
More than half a million soldiers fought there over four days. It was the largest battle in Europe until the First World War. They called it the Battle of the Nations.
Napoleon lost.
In 1814, the allies invaded France itself. Napoleon fought a brilliant defensive campaign, winning battle after battle against much larger armies.
It was not enough.
On the thirty-first of March, 1814, the allies marched into Paris. His own marshals told him it was over.
On the sixth of April, Napoleon abdicated.
He was sent into exile on Elba, a tiny island off the coast of Italy. He was allowed to keep the title of Emperor, but his empire was now a few square miles of rock.
The old French royal family returned. Louis the Eighteenth, brother of the executed king, took the throne.
The great powers gathered at the Congress of Vienna to redraw the map of Europe.
They thought the story was finished.

Less than a year later, in February 1815, Napoleon escaped from Elba.
He landed in the south of France with around a thousand men.
Near Grenoble, a regiment of royal soldiers blocked the road. Napoleon walked out in front of them, alone, and opened his coat.
"If there is one among you who wants to kill his Emperor," he said, "here I am."
The soldiers did not fire. They cheered. And they joined him.
On the twentieth of March, he entered Paris. The king had fled.
Europe declared him an outlaw. Huge armies began to gather against him.
Napoleon decided to strike first, in Belgium, before they could unite.
On the sixteenth of June, he beat the Prussians at Ligny. But he did not destroy them.
Two days later came Waterloo.
Because of the rain, Napoleon delayed the attack until late morning to let the ground dry.
Every hour mattered, because the Prussians were marching to help Wellington.
The French attacked the farm of Hougoumont. They threw massive columns of infantry at Wellington's centre. Marshal Ney led wave after wave of cavalry against British squares.
The squares held.
In the late afternoon, the Prussians arrived on Napoleon's flank.
In the evening, Napoleon sent in his last reserve, the legendary Imperial Guard, which had almost never been beaten.
It was driven back.
A cry went through the French ranks: "The Guard retreats!"
And the French army broke.
Waterloo was over. So were the Hundred Days.
Napoleon abdicated for the second time. This time, the British sent him far away, to Saint Helena, a lonely island in the South Atlantic.
He spent his last years there, dictating his memoirs and shaping his own legend.
He died on the fifth of May, 1821. He was fifty-one years old.

The Napoleonic Wars cost millions of lives. Historians estimate somewhere between three and six million soldiers and civilians died.
But they also changed the world.
The old Holy Roman Empire was gone. Ideas of nationalism had spread to Germany, Italy, Poland and beyond. His legal code survives in countries he never ruled.
And after Waterloo, Britain stood as the world's leading power for a century.
The Congress of Vienna built a new balance of power. Europe would not see another war on this scale for nearly a hundred years.
Napoleon remains one of the most debated figures in history.
A genius of war. A reformer of law. A dictator who crowned himself. A man whose ambition built an empire, and then destroyed it.
He once said: "Impossible is a word to be found only in the dictionary of fools."
For twenty years, he made the impossible look easy.
And then, on a muddy field in Belgium, the impossible caught up with him.
