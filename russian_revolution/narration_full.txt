RUSSIAN REVOLUTION - FULL NARRATION (~30 min)

===== PART 1 (~15 min) =====

Yekaterinburg, Russia. The night of the sixteenth of July, 1918.
It is past midnight. In a large house with whitewashed windows, a family is woken up and told to get dressed.
There is fighting nearby, the guards tell them. For their own safety, they must move to the basement.
A father carries his sick thirteen-year-old son down the stairs. His wife follows. Then four young daughters, their family doctor, and three loyal servants.
They are told to wait. Chairs are brought for the mother and the boy.
The father is a quiet, polite man with a neat beard. Just eighteen months earlier, he ruled one-sixth of the Earth's land surface. He commanded the largest army in the world. More than a hundred and seventy million people called him "Little Father."
His name is Nicholas Romanov. Tsar Nicholas the Second, the last Emperor of Russia.
And the men who are about to walk into that basement are not there to protect him.
How did the most powerful monarch in the world end up in a cellar in the Ural Mountains, with no throne, no army, and no friends?
How did a country of peasants and priests become the first communist state in history?
This is the story of the Russian Revolution.

To understand how it happened, we have to go back to the beginning of the twentieth century, to an empire that seemed eternal.
The Romanov family had ruled Russia since 1613. For three hundred years, the Tsar was not just a king. He was believed to be chosen by God. His word was law. There was no parliament, no elections, and no free press.
Russia was enormous. It stretched from Poland to the Pacific Ocean, across eleven time zones. But it was also deeply poor and deeply unequal.
Around eighty percent of the people were peasants. Until 1861, many of them had been serfs, owned by landlords and bought and sold along with the land. Even after they were freed, most still lived in wooden huts, worked tiny plots of land, and could not read or write.
At the very top sat a tiny aristocracy, living in palaces of gold and marble in St. Petersburg, speaking French at dinner, and dancing at balls that lasted until dawn.
In the cities, a new class was growing. Factory workers. Millions of them, working twelve or fourteen hours a day in dangerous, filthy conditions, sleeping in crowded barracks.
They were angry. And a few dangerous new ideas were starting to spread among them.

In 1894, a twenty-six-year-old man became Tsar. Nicholas the Second.
He was gentle, devoted to his family, and deeply religious. But he had never wanted to rule. He once admitted, "I am not prepared to be a Tsar. I know nothing of the business of ruling."
His reign began with a terrible omen.
In May 1896, to celebrate his coronation, free gifts were promised to the people of Moscow: bread, sausage, and a souvenir cup. Hundreds of thousands gathered on a field called Khodynka.
Rumours spread that there were not enough gifts for everyone. The crowd surged forward. People fell into ditches and were trampled.
More than a thousand people died.
That same evening, Nicholas attended a grand ball at the French embassy, as if nothing had happened.
Many Russians never forgot it.

Nicholas married a German princess, Alexandra. She was shy, proud, and fiercely protective of her husband's absolute power. Together they had four daughters: Olga, Tatiana, Maria and Anastasia.
But Russia's law said that only a son could inherit the throne.
Finally, in 1904, a boy was born. Alexei. The heir.
The joy did not last. Within weeks, the doctors discovered that Alexei had haemophilia, a disease that stops the blood from clotting. A simple fall or bruise could cause bleeding that would not stop. Any accident could kill him.
The illness was kept a state secret. And it would lead the royal family into the arms of one of the strangest men in history.
But first, the empire was about to be humiliated.

In 1904, Russia went to war with Japan over territory in Manchuria and Korea.
The Russian court laughed at the idea of losing to a small Asian nation. Nicholas called the Japanese "monkeys."
It was a disaster.
The Japanese besieged and captured the Russian naval base at Port Arthur. Russia then sent its Baltic Fleet on an incredible journey, eighteen thousand miles, halfway around the world.
When the fleet finally arrived, in May 1905, at the Tsushima Strait, the Japanese navy destroyed it in a single afternoon. Almost the entire fleet was sunk or captured.
For the first time in modern history, an Asian power had crushed a European empire.
At home, the anger exploded.

On a freezing Sunday in January 1905, a priest named Father Georgy Gapon led a huge peaceful march through St. Petersburg.
Tens of thousands of workers and their families walked toward the Winter Palace. They carried religious icons and portraits of the Tsar. They sang hymns. They wanted to hand him a petition asking for an eight-hour working day, fair wages, and a parliament.
They believed that if only the "Little Father" knew how they suffered, he would help them.
Nicholas was not even in the city.
As the crowds approached, soldiers opened fire.
Hundreds of people were killed in the snow, including women and children.
It became known as Bloody Sunday.
That day, something broke. Millions of ordinary Russians stopped believing that the Tsar was on their side.

The year 1905 turned into a revolution.
Workers went on strike across the country. Peasants burned their landlords' manor houses. On the Black Sea, the sailors of the battleship Potemkin mutinied, threw their officers overboard and raised the red flag.
In St. Petersburg, workers formed a new kind of council to organise the strikes. They called it a "soviet." One of its leaders was a brilliant young revolutionary named Leon Trotsky.
Remember that name.
Terrified, Nicholas gave in. In October 1905, he issued a manifesto promising civil rights and an elected parliament, called the Duma.
For a moment, it looked as if Russia might become a modern constitutional monarchy.
But Nicholas never truly accepted it. Within a few years, he had stripped the Duma of most of its power.
The revolution had been crushed. But the anger had not gone away. It had only gone underground.

Meanwhile, inside the palace, the Tsarina was desperate.
Doctors could do nothing for Alexei. Every time he bled, he screamed in pain for days, and his parents feared he would die.
Then, around 1907, a strange visitor arrived at court.
He was a wandering holy man from Siberia, with long greasy hair, a wild beard, and piercing pale eyes. He rarely washed. He drank heavily. He was rumoured to have endless affairs.
His name was Grigori Rasputin.
And somehow, when Rasputin prayed beside the boy's bed, Alexei's bleeding seemed to stop.
Historians still argue about how. Perhaps his calm voice relaxed the boy. Perhaps he told the doctors to stop giving aspirin, which made the bleeding worse. Perhaps it was simply luck.
But to Alexandra, Rasputin was a miracle sent by God. And he soon had more influence over the royal family than any minister.
To the rest of Russia, it looked like the empire was being controlled by a drunken peasant mystic.

There was one man who tried to save the system from within. The Prime Minister, Pyotr Stolypin.
He crushed revolutionaries without mercy. So many were hanged that the noose became known as "Stolypin's necktie."
But he also tried to give peasants their own land, hoping to create a class of loyal, prosperous farmers. He said Russia needed twenty years of peace to succeed.
It never got them.
In 1911, Stolypin was shot dead at the opera in Kiev, in front of the Tsar himself.
Three years later, Russia was at war again.

In August 1914, the First World War broke out.
At first, Russians rallied around their Tsar. Huge crowds knelt in front of the Winter Palace and sang "God Save the Tsar." St. Petersburg was even renamed Petrograd, because its old name sounded too German.
But Russia was not ready for modern war.
In the very first month, at the Battle of Tannenberg, an entire Russian army was surrounded and destroyed. Its commander took his own life.
Soldiers were sent to the front without boots. In some units, there was only one rifle for every two or three men, and soldiers were told to pick up the weapons of the dead.
By 1915, millions of Russians had been killed, wounded or captured.
Then Nicholas made a fateful decision. He travelled to the front and took personal command of the army.
Now, every defeat was his defeat.
And back in Petrograd, he left the government in the hands of his German-born wife, the most unpopular woman in Russia, and her adviser, Rasputin.
Ministers were hired and fired at Rasputin's suggestion. Rumours spread that the Tsarina was a German spy. They were false, but millions believed them.

By late 1916, even the aristocracy had had enough.
On a December night, a group of nobles led by Prince Felix Yusupov, one of the richest men in Russia, invited Rasputin to his palace.
According to the legend, they fed him cakes and wine laced with enough poison to kill several men. Nothing happened. Yusupov shot him. Rasputin seemed to die, then suddenly leapt up and grabbed him. He was shot again, beaten, wrapped up and thrown through a hole in the ice of the frozen river.
Historians today believe much of this story was exaggerated. But one thing is certain: Rasputin was dead.
The nobles thought they had saved the monarchy.
They were too late.

The winter of 1917 was brutally cold.
The war had wrecked Russia's railways. Food and fuel could not reach the cities. In Petrograd, women stood in bread queues for hours in temperatures of minus twenty, often to find the shelves empty.
On the twenty-third of February, 1917, by the Russian calendar, it was International Women's Day.
Thousands of women textile workers walked out of their factories and marched through the streets, shouting one word: "Bread!"
The next day, they were joined by striking men. Within three days, two hundred thousand workers filled the streets of the capital. Their banners now said: "Down with the war!" and "Down with the Tsar!"
The government ordered the army to restore order.
And then, the unthinkable happened.
The soldiers refused.
Many of them were young peasants, recently conscripted, and they had no wish to shoot hungry women. One regiment after another mutinied. Soldiers shot their own officers, opened the arsenals and handed rifles to the crowds. Prisons were thrown open. Police stations burned.
In a few days, the Tsar's government in Petrograd simply ceased to exist.

Nicholas was hundreds of miles away at army headquarters. He tried to return to the capital by train, but rebel railway workers blocked the line.
His train was stopped near the city of Pskov.
There, his own generals told him the truth. The army would no longer follow him. The only way to save Russia, and perhaps the dynasty, was to step down.
On the second of March, 1917, in a railway carriage, Nicholas the Second signed his abdication.
He gave up the throne for himself and for his sick son, in favour of his brother, Grand Duke Michael.
The next day, Michael refused the crown.
Just like that, after more than three hundred years, the Romanov dynasty was over.
That night, Nicholas wrote in his diary: "All around me there is treason, cowardice and deceit."

Russia was free. Overnight, it became, on paper, one of the freest countries in the world.
Two new powers rose to fill the empty throne: a Provisional Government of liberal politicians, and the Petrograd Soviet, the council of workers and soldiers.
They promised elections, freedom of speech, and a democratic future.
But they made one fatal mistake. They decided to keep fighting the war.
And far away, in a small apartment in Zurich, Switzerland, a bald, middle-aged exile read the news of the revolution with burning excitement.
He had spent most of his life plotting the end of the Tsar.
Now he needed to get home.
And Russia's greatest enemy, Germany, was about to give him a ride.

===== PART 2 (~15 min) =====

His name was Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov. The world would know him as Lenin.
He was the son of a school inspector. When he was seventeen, his older brother Alexander was hanged for plotting to assassinate the Tsar. Lenin never forgave the system.
He became a follower of Karl Marx, the German philosopher who predicted that workers would one day rise up, overthrow the rich, and create a society without classes, without private property, and without exploitation.
But Lenin added something of his own. He believed the workers would not do it alone. They needed to be led by a small, disciplined party of professional revolutionaries.
His party was called the Bolsheviks, which means "the majority," even though for most of their history, they were a tiny minority.

In April 1917, the German government made a cold calculation.
If they sent Lenin back to Russia, he might cause so much chaos that Russia would pull out of the war.
So they put Lenin and about thirty other revolutionaries on a train across Germany. The carriage was declared "sealed," so that no one would get on or off on German soil. Winston Churchill later wrote that the Germans sent Lenin into Russia "like a plague bacillus."
On the night of the third of April, Lenin arrived at the Finland Station in Petrograd.
A huge crowd was waiting with red flags and a military band. Lenin climbed onto an armoured car, lit by searchlights, and gave a speech that shocked even his own followers.
No cooperation with the Provisional Government. No more war. All power to the soviets.
His slogans were simple, and they were exactly what millions of Russians wanted to hear.
"Peace. Land. Bread."

Over the summer, the Provisional Government lost control.
A young, dramatic lawyer named Alexander Kerensky became its leading figure, first as minister of war, then as prime minister. He was a brilliant speaker, but he made a terrible decision. In June 1917, he launched a new offensive against Germany.
It collapsed. Soldiers deserted by the hundreds of thousands. Many walked home with their rifles to seize land from their landlords.
In July, armed crowds of workers and sailors poured into the streets of Petrograd, demanding that the soviets take power. The uprising failed. The government accused Lenin of being a German agent, and ordered his arrest.
Lenin shaved off his beard, put on a wig, and escaped to Finland, disguised as a railway worker. For a few weeks, it looked as if the Bolsheviks were finished.
Then, in late August, the army's commander-in-chief, General Lavr Kornilov, marched troops toward Petrograd. Many believed he wanted to crush the revolution and become a military dictator.
In panic, Kerensky turned to the only people who could defend the city: the soviets, and the Bolsheviks. He released Bolshevik leaders from prison and handed out rifles to their workers' militia, the Red Guards.
Kornilov's coup collapsed without a fight.
But now the Bolsheviks were armed, organised and more popular than ever. And Kerensky had made himself look weak to everyone.
Lenin, still in hiding, sent letter after letter to his comrades.
The time had come. They had to seize power. Now.

Here is a strange fact. The October Revolution actually happened in November.
At the time, Russia still used the old Julian calendar, which was thirteen days behind the rest of the world. So the twenty-fifth of October in Russia was the seventh of November everywhere else.
On the night of the twenty-fourth of October, 1917, Red Guards and soldiers loyal to the Bolsheviks, organised by Leon Trotsky, quietly took control of Petrograd. Bridges. Railway stations. The telephone exchange. The post office. The state bank.
There was almost no resistance.
The next evening, the cruiser Aurora, anchored in the river, fired a single blank shot as a signal. Bolshevik forces moved into the Winter Palace, where the last ministers of the Provisional Government were hiding.
In later Soviet films, this was shown as a heroic battle, with thousands of people charging through the gates under heavy fire.
In reality, the palace was defended by a few hundred cadets, a group of Cossacks and a women's battalion. Most of them had already gone home. The attackers wandered through the endless corridors, got lost, and some of them broke into the Tsar's wine cellars.
Only a handful of people were killed. Kerensky had already escaped in a car borrowed from the American embassy.
In just one night, with almost no bloodshed, the Bolsheviks had taken control of the capital of the largest country on Earth.
The bloodshed would come later.

Lenin moved fast.
He announced that Russia would leave the war. Land was taken from the nobles, the Church and the Crown, and given to the peasants. Factories were placed under workers' control. Banks were nationalised.
But one thing stood in his way.
For months, every party had promised free elections for a Constituent Assembly, a parliament that would decide Russia's future. The elections went ahead in November 1917.
The Bolsheviks lost. They won only about a quarter of the votes. The peasant party, the Socialist Revolutionaries, won far more.
When the Assembly met in January 1918, Lenin's guards closed it down after just one day. The next morning, the deputies found the doors locked.
Russia's first truly democratic parliament had lasted less than twenty-four hours.
Lenin also created a new secret police, the Cheka. Its job was to find and destroy enemies of the revolution. It would become one of the most feared organisations in history.

Lenin had promised peace. Now he had to pay for it.
In March 1918, at the town of Brest-Litovsk, Russia signed a peace treaty with Germany. The price was enormous. Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland and Ukraine. It lost around a third of its population, much of its farmland, and most of its coal and iron.
Many Russians, even many Bolsheviks, saw it as a betrayal.
To be safer from the Germans, Lenin moved the capital from Petrograd to Moscow, where it has remained ever since.
And then Russia exploded into civil war.

On one side were the Bolsheviks, now called the Reds.
On the other side were the Whites, a mix of former Tsarist officers, landowners, liberals, Cossacks, and even rival socialists. They had little in common, except hatred of Lenin.
Foreign powers joined in too. Britain, France, the United States and Japan sent troops to Russian ports, hoping to keep Russia in the war against Germany and to stop communism from spreading.
At one point, the Reds controlled only a small heartland around Moscow and Petrograd, surrounded on all sides.
The man who saved them was Leon Trotsky.
He built the Red Army from almost nothing, into an army of millions. He used former Tsarist officers, but watched them closely, sometimes holding their families hostage to make sure they stayed loyal. He travelled from front to front in an armoured train, with its own printing press, radio station and band, appearing wherever the fighting was most desperate.

In the summer of 1918, a White army, including a legion of Czech soldiers who had turned against the Bolsheviks, was advancing on the city of Yekaterinburg.
In that city, in a house now called the "House of Special Purpose," the Bolsheviks were holding the former Tsar and his family.
Which brings us back to where our story began.
On the night of the sixteenth to the seventeenth of July, 1918, the family was taken down to the basement. A guard read out a short statement. The Ural Soviet had sentenced them to death.
Nicholas, Alexandra, their son Alexei, their four daughters, their doctor and three servants were all killed.
The daughters had sewn diamonds and jewels into their clothing to hide them. The bodies were taken to a forest and hidden.
For decades, the Soviet Union kept the details secret.
Because the bodies were never shown, rumours spread that one of the daughters, Anastasia, had survived. Several women claimed to be her. The most famous, Anna Anderson, convinced many people for over sixty years.
But in 1991, after the fall of the Soviet Union, the remains were dug up from the forest. The last two missing bodies were found in 2007. DNA tests proved that the whole family had died that night. Anna Anderson was not Anastasia.
In 2000, the Russian Orthodox Church declared Nicholas and his family saints.

A few weeks after the murder of the Tsar, in August 1918, a young woman named Fanny Kaplan shot Lenin twice as he left a factory in Moscow. He survived, but one bullet stayed lodged in his neck for almost four years.
The Bolsheviks answered with the Red Terror. The Cheka arrested, imprisoned and executed tens of thousands of people suspected of opposing them. Nobles, priests, officers, merchants and ordinary peasants who refused to hand over their grain.
The Whites carried out their own massacres and pogroms. Both sides committed terrible atrocities.
To feed the army and the cities, the Bolsheviks introduced "War Communism." Grain was taken from peasants by force. Private trade was banned. Money almost disappeared.
The Reds won the civil war, slowly, battle by battle. By 1920, the last White armies had been defeated or had fled across the Black Sea.
But the country they had won was in ruins.

Between war, terror, disease and hunger, somewhere between seven and twelve million people died during the civil war years.
In 1921, a terrible famine struck the Volga region. Around five million people starved to death. Russia had to accept food aid from the United States.
That same year, the sailors of the naval fortress of Kronstadt, once the proudest heroes of the revolution, rose in rebellion. They demanded free elections and free speech, and called for "soviets without Bolsheviks."
Trotsky's Red Army crossed the frozen sea and crushed them.
Lenin realised he had pushed the country too far. He introduced a New Economic Policy, allowing peasants and small traders to sell their goods for profit again. For a few years, the economy began to recover.
On the thirtieth of December, 1922, a new country was officially born: the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. The USSR.

But Lenin would not live to see it grow.
In 1922, he suffered the first of several strokes. Paralysed and unable to speak properly, he watched from his bed as his comrades began to fight over who would succeed him.
Before he lost his voice, he dictated a secret letter, later known as his "Testament." In it, he warned about one man in particular. A quiet, patient Georgian, who controlled the party's appointments as its General Secretary.
"Stalin is too rude," Lenin wrote. He urged the party to find a way to remove him.
Lenin died on the twenty-first of January, 1924. He was fifty-three years old.
Against his family's wishes, his body was embalmed and put on display in a mausoleum in Red Square, where it can still be seen today.
His warning about Stalin was quietly hidden away.

Most people expected Trotsky, the hero of the revolution and the creator of the Red Army, to take over.
But Joseph Stalin was a master of patience and political manoeuvre. He placed his own loyal supporters in key positions. He played his rivals against each other, one by one.
Trotsky was pushed out of the government, then out of the party, then out of the country.
In 1940, in exile in Mexico, Trotsky was killed by a Soviet agent with an ice axe.
By then, Stalin ruled the Soviet Union with absolute power, greater than any Tsar had ever had.

The Russian Revolution promised freedom, equality and bread.
For millions, it brought the opposite: civil war, famine, secret police and the labour camps of the Gulag.
And yet, it also changed the world like few events in history.
The Soviet Union it created would defeat Hitler in the Second World War, put the first human in space, and face the United States in a Cold War that brought the world to the edge of nuclear destruction.
Communist revolutions would follow in China, Vietnam, Cuba and beyond. For much of the twentieth century, one third of humanity lived under governments inspired by what happened in Petrograd in 1917.
Then, on the twenty-fifth of December, 1991, the red flag was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time. The Soviet Union was gone.
The empire of the Tsars had lasted three hundred years.
The state built by Lenin lasted seventy-four.
It all began with women in a bread queue, shouting a single word in the freezing streets of Petrograd.
"Bread."
This was the Russian Revolution.
